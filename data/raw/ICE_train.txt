InterCity-Express (ICE)

InterCity-Express, branded as ICE, is the high-speed rail service operated by Deutsche Bahn, forming the top tier of long-distance passenger rail in Germany. The first ICE services began in 1991, connecting major cities such as Hamburg, Hannover, Frankfurt, and Munich, and the network has since expanded to link nearly all large German cities as well as international destinations including Vienna, Zurich, Amsterdam, Brussels, and Paris through cooperation with neighboring rail operators.

ICE trains operate at speeds of up to 300 kilometers per hour on dedicated high-speed lines, such as the Cologne-Frankfurt route, though on many older or shared sections of track the trains run at lower conventional speeds, which is one reason average journey speeds are often considerably below the trains' theoretical top speed. Several generations of ICE rolling stock have been deployed over the decades, with the newest generation, the ICE 4, designed for greater capacity and modularity, entering service starting in 2017 and gradually becoming the backbone of the long-distance fleet.

A defining feature of ICE service is its integration with the broader European high-speed network. Through joint ventures and bilateral agreements, ICE trains run through services into France in partnership with the French operator SNCF, and into the Netherlands and Belgium, allowing passengers to travel between Frankfurt and Amsterdam, for example, without changing trains. Ticket pricing for ICE travel is dynamic, generally cheaper the earlier a ticket is booked, with a flat-rate flexible fare available for last-minute or unplanned travel at a significantly higher cost than advance-purchase fares.

Reliability has been a persistent challenge for ICE services specifically because they share track with slower regional and freight trains across much of the network, meaning delays cascade easily from one service to another. DB has cited capacity constraints on key corridors, particularly around Frankfurt, Cologne, and Mannheim, as a structural cause of recurring ICE delays, which the ongoing Generalsanierung renovation program aims to partially resolve by rebuilding bottleneck sections with greater capacity.
