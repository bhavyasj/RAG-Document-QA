Rail transport in Germany

Germany has one of the most extensive and heavily used rail networks in Europe, shaped historically by the country's industrial geography and, since reunification, by the integration of separate East and West German systems into a single network. The network totals roughly 33,000 kilometers of track, a substantial portion of which is electrified, and it is overseen at the infrastructure level by DB InfraGO, a subsidiary of Deutsche Bahn responsible for track, signaling, and station maintenance, while train operations are carried out both by Deutsche Bahn itself and by a growing number of private and regional competitors.

Germany's federal structure means that regional rail services, including Regional-Express and Regionalbahn trains as well as most S-Bahn networks, are commissioned and subsidized not by the federal government directly but by the sixteen federal states, often through regional transport authorities that put service contracts out to competitive tender. This has resulted in a mixed-operator landscape where companies such as Transdev, Netinera, and Abellio operate regional trains under contract alongside DB Regio, DB's own regional arm.

Long-distance rail, by contrast, remains almost entirely a Deutsche Bahn monopoly in practice, although the market has technically been open to competition since European Union rail liberalization directives took effect in the 2000s. A small number of competitors, most notably the private operator FlixTrain, have entered the long-distance market on selected routes, typically offering lower fares than DB's ICE and IC services but with less frequent departures and fewer route options.

Freight rail in Germany carries a significant share of the country's goods transport, particularly for bulk commodities like coal, chemicals, and automotive parts, and German freight rail connects seamlessly to major seaports such as Hamburg and Bremerhaven as well as to neighboring countries' networks, making Germany a key transit corridor for European rail freight moving between northern ports and southern and eastern Europe.

Investment in the network has become a major political issue, with successive German governments pledging large multi-year infrastructure spending packages aimed at reducing the maintenance backlog that built up over decades of underinvestment, a backlog widely cited by transport researchers and DB itself as the principal cause of declining punctuality across the network since the 2010s.
